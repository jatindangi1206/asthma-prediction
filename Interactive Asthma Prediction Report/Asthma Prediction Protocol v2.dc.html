Signal processing

raw · smoothing · residual · surprise · CPD · embeddings

Each channel branches into several derived signals. Every branch is a phase a stage that runs once, over the whole cohort, and stores what it produced. The paths then pick from the shelf; nothing is recomputed per path, per model or per fold.

3.1 Smoothing and residuals
raw signal→ smoother (10 to choose from)→ smoothed · the baseline→ residual · raw − smoothed
schematic — not patient data

Each channel yields three signals, not one — raw, the estimated baseline, and the residual. All three always travel into the model, and so do temp, steps and sleep, unsmoothed. What a path chooses is not which signal the model sees; it is which one the change-point detectors read.

3.3 Surprise — forecast error, from a frozen model

Both 3.3 and 3.4 replace a hand-built quantity with something a pretrained model computes; neither is trained on this cohort. A frozen forecaster is shown the history (~3.5 days) and asked what comes next (the next hours). The surprise is how wrong it was.

surprise = 89 − 65 = +24 · tail ∈ [0, 1]

A smoother would absorb this — it is built to track a drifting baseline, so after two or three high readings its baseline rises and the residual shrinks. A forecaster predicting from before the change does not. It also reports where 89 fell in its own predictive distribution (tail, 0 to 1), which is comparable across patients in a way "+24" is not: 24 means different things at a 30 and a 90 baseline.

The only clean residual in the pipeline. Frozen weights, nothing fitted on the cohort, each block predicted from strictly earlier readings — so unlike nine of the ten smoothers there is no lookahead to argue about.

3.4 Embedding — learned representation
7-day window→frozen encoder · MOMENT / MantisV2→512 numbers / day→model

Instead of choosing and relying on mean, median, SD, min and max, the encoder learns a representation of the entire temporal pattern. The 512 numbers are reduced inside the training fold, never before it — reducing earlier would fit on test patients.

The context budget · days × profile = input length
512 slots · {{ budDays }} day(s)≈ {{ budMin }} min / slot

MOMENT is fixed at 512, MantisV2 wants a multiple of 32 and was pretrained at 512, Timestral takes up to 32,768. So against a fixed ceiling, changing --days does not change how much the model is handed — it changes the resolution those slots are spent at. 7 days into 512 is ~20 minutes a slot; 1 day is ~2.8 minutes. HRV's 7-day window is 385 points and fits natively; HR's is 5,208 and needs --profile 73. windowing.bins_for(7, 512) returns 73, so the arithmetic is never implicit.

Why one score is not enough

Only level error can be used alone. Every other score is maxed by a useless smoother:

useless smootherperfect onzero on
Do nothing (x̂ = x)correlation, peak preservationvariance and roughness reduction
Flat line (x̂ = the average)variance and roughness reductioncorrelation, peak preservation

So scores are combined in pairs that pull against each other (reductions as fractions, 0 to 1):

R remove = √( variance_reduction × roughness_reduction )
K keep   = √( peak_preservation × correlation )
S joint  = √( R × K )

A square root of a product is 0 if either part is 0, so both useless smoothers score 0. Level error is kept separate: shifting the level never buys anything, so it should not be traded against the others.

Intervals
95 % intervals come from resampling test patients — not days.

Days within a patient are dependent, and resampling them gives an interval several times too narrow. The five primary metrics carry an interval; lead time and detection delay are medians over caught episodes and are reported as point estimates.

The bootstrap is ~85 % of a full grid's wall clock (1000 draws: 2.9 s per evaluation). --boot 0 skips it and is 145× quicker — iterate with it, turn it on once for the final tables.

These judge a smoother directly against the raw trace, with no model and no labels. Averaged per patient and then across patients, so one long recording cannot dominate — and they are the only scores available for a patient with no exacerbation record. Notation: x = the raw HRV reading, x̂ = the smoothed value a method gives.

Peak preservation, step by step — the most important check. If an exacerbation shows up as a burst in HRV, a smoother that flattens bursts can score well on variance and roughness but is useless for early warning. Physiologically we don't know what is pathological for HRV, so we keep away from assuming smoothed HRV is a better predictor than raw. (1) Find peaks p in the raw signal: points that stand out by at least half the patient's own spread. (2) Look 6 readings before and after each (about 2 hours). (3) Rise = peak value minus the lowest value in that window, for raw and for smoothed. (4) ratiop = clip(smoothed rise / raw rise, 0, 1): 1 = kept fully, 0 = flattened. (5) Take the median over all peaks, so one huge spike can't dominate.

Variance asks "how far from the average?"; roughness asks "how far from the previous reading?". A slow drift has high variance but low roughness; fast jitter is the opposite. Round-1 scores → §3.5.

7.1 Findings · why a foundation model is not simply used as the predictor

The obvious move is to hand the raw series to a large pretrained model and let it predict. That is not the main approach here, for reasons worth stating because they will be asked.

Pretraining transfers the wrong knowledge

Pretraining teaches a model what time series look like — trends, rhythms, bursts, regime changes. It teaches it nothing about what an asthma exacerbation looks like. That mapping has to be learned from the labels, and there are 144 positive days (§2). So the job splits by where the information comes from: signal knowledge from the big frozen model, which needs no labels; disease knowledge from a small classifier, which must learn a 144-example mapping and does so better for being small.

"Direct" is mostly the same thing in disguise

144 positives cannot fine-tune a 110M–385M parameter body. So "use MOMENT directly" necessarily means freeze the body and train a small head — which is the embedding route (§3.4), with the head restricted to one linear layer. Running embeddings into xgboost is strictly more general.

7.3 Limitations
Test sets vary between seeds

The test positives nearly double between seeds — 19 on seed 1 against 35 on seed 2 for detection, 23 against 41 for onset. Whole patients move, and an exacerbator carries all of their days with them. So part of the spread between seeds is the test set being easier or harder, not the model changing. This is why a result is read as a mean across the five with its interval, never from one seed, and why the run asserts that every model in a fold saw identical test days, positives, episodes and patients. Evidence: §5.4

Small number of episodes

57 scoreable episodes, about 10 per test set, against several hundred numbers — some will separate by chance. Pre-register one primary comparison. The paediatric group has 8 exacerbation days against 125 among the adults, so the paediatric interval will be wide. Evidence: §2, §6

Lookahead in nine smoothers

Nine of ten smoothers set per-patient settings from the whole recording; residuals from them are optimistically biased for onset. Only anrewma (and the frozen-forecaster surprise) is clean. The round-1 leader, rspf, has the largest truncation deviation of all ten (3.16); for real-time use its settings must be fitted only on past data. Evidence: §3.2–3.3, §3.5

Smoother choice is label-free so far

Round 1 compares smoothed to raw only; it does not show which smoother helps predict exacerbations. The top four by S (kim, krlst, rspf, gpssm) sit within 1.2 % and swap by cohort, so the final pick waits for the model round. Evidence: §3.5

Sparse channels

HRV covers 6.1 %, temp 8.0 %, steps 2.8 %; spo2 is left out with 21 readings in the whole cohort. HRV segments (49 readings) cannot fill a 512-point context, so foundation routes run on HR. Sleep stages carry no timing. Evidence: §2, §3.6

The raw baseline comes first. Nothing else counts as an improvement until path 1's number exists, and always_no fixes what every other number means. §4, §5.5
